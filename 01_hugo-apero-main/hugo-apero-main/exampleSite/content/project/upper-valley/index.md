question: "How does the Upper Valley’s (NH–VT) trade labor shortage contribute to home unaffordability, and how is that shortage experienced by its workers?"
draft_pdf: documents/papers/SEA.pdf
abstract: |
  This paper is an ethnography of the housing production and regulation sector in New Hampshire and Vermont’s Upper Valley region, particularly its building sector. Drawing on ethnographic fieldwork throughout my senior year of college, interviews, local history, and descriptive quantitative evidence, the paper treats the Upper Valley as a regional manifestation of a national housing failure. It argues that the region’s estate is not building enough, and that a relevant reason why is a trade labor shortage where aspirations for a good life compete against felt constraints. There, builders’ predominant aspiration of working knowledge for the good competes against felt constraints, namely around housing and wages, pedagogy, and workers’ bodies, pushing people away from the trades through the very aspiration which pulled them in.

        <p class='story-kicker'>1. Failures in dwelling</p>
        <h2>It was all there at Bear Ridge, even though I didn't know it</h2>
        <p>At Bear Ridge Speedway, cars pass with a force the body can feel and mud rises from the starting line at the region's last remaining track for its grassroots sport. There, among MAGA hats and cigarettes, people spoke to me about a sport that had become more expensive, about being pushed out, and about tracks that were now Burger Kings. Yet when I asked people what worried them most, it was housing costs. Following Liberation Theology, I set out to study those costs from the periphery alongside one of their drivers, second only to zoning: a trade labor shortage. It was all there at the speedway—home unaffordability, manual labor, and the thinning of certain kinds of work and futures in the Upper Valley.</p>

        <p class='story-kicker'>1.1 Setting</p>
        <h2>An uneven regional geography</h2>
        <p>The research took place in the Upper Connecticut River Valley between New Hampshire and Vermont, on Western Abenaki homelands. Far from its days as hunting grounds, then agriculture, sheep grazing, and manufacturing, its economic geography is now organized around healthcare and educational services in a triangle formed by Interstates 89 and 91. In the paper's NHGIS decennial Census reconstruction, manufacturing was the region's second-largest industry behind retail in 1980, at approximately 22.9 percent of employment. By 2000 it had fallen to approximately 13.0 percent and been overtaken by health and education, while the towns furthest from the intersection of the interstates have hollowed out.</p>

      <p class='story-kicker'>2. Economic matters and method</p>
      <h2>The estate is not building enough</h2>
      <p>I use <em>oikos</em>, household or estate, to name the bounded but connected space of housing production and regulation in which I did my fieldwork and came to focus on its builders. If, for the ancient Greeks, economics (<em>οἰκονομία</em>) concerns the management (<em>nemein</em>) of the household or estate (<em>oikos</em>), then to say something has gone wrong in the region's economics of housing is to say something has gone wrong in the management of its <em>estate</em>. What does this oikos look like, and what is being mismanaged? During the fall and winter of my senior year, I observed, participated, looked to quantify, and conducted 25 life-history interviews with people across the estate. I spent time helping with small builds, attending zoning meetings, and working as an assistant teacher at a trade school. Three central economic facts reconstructed from Census and ACS data are crucial to explaining the lived experiences I encountered.</p>
      <p class='story-anonymity-note'><em>People and company names have been anonymized; town names have not.</em></p>
    </div>
    <div class='story-wide-figure story-wide-figure--estate'>

    <div class='story-method-cards' aria-label='Three descriptive regional facts'>
      <article class='story-method-card'>
        <p class='story-method-card__type'>First fact</p>
        <h3>Housing prices and incomes have pulled apart</h3>
        <p>The Upper Valley proxy mirrors the national “great divorce”: housing costs and home values rise faster than household income. Behind the line are rents that come due, households that move away, and futures that become harder to keep.</p>
        {{< story-image src="img/projects/upper-valley/housing-affordability.png" alt="Chart of home unaffordability and housing supply in the Upper Valley" caption="Housing costs separating from household income in the Upper Valley proxy." >}}
      </article>
      <article class='story-method-card'>
        <p class='story-method-card__type'>Second fact</p>
        <h3>A secular shortage of housing supply</h3>
        <p>Housing-stock growth fell from the rates of the 1970s and 1980s to 0.4 percent in the 1990s, recovered during the housing bubble, then approached zero again. The estate has not built enough homes.</p>
        {{< story-image src="img/projects/upper-valley/housing-affordability.png" alt="Chart showing the slowing growth of the Upper Valley housing stock" caption="The secular slowdown in housing-stock growth in the Upper Valley proxy." >}}
      </article>
      <article class='story-method-card'>
        <p class='story-method-card__type'>Third fact</p>
        <h3>Trade labor is one part of the constraint</h3>
        <p>After the 2008 housing bubble, the number of local construction workers fell by roughly 30 percent and remains far below its peak even as housing prices and construction demand have risen. Builders described the shortage as a coordination problem that delays every stage of a project.</p>
        {{< story-image src="img/projects/upper-valley/construction-workers.png" alt="Charts showing construction labor in Grafton and Windsor counties" caption="The decline in local construction employment in the project's two-county proxy." >}}

<div class='story-chapter__heading'>
<p class='story-kicker'>3. The Good and Its Constraints</p>
<p>Nearly everyone had an eager answer; I was told it was because the work ethic was gone, there weren't any loyalties anymore, that younger generations were trapped by computers, and the trades were looked down upon. In what follows, rather than adjudicating these as separate explanations, I treat them as shared symptoms of a deeper struggle over the “good” and what kind of work can still promise it. In doing so, I follow a call for an anthropology of the good (Fischer, 2014; Robbins, 2013).</p>
</div>
<div class='story-method-cards story-method-cards--three' aria-label='Three fieldwork vignettes'>
<article class='story-method-card'>
<p class='story-method-card__type'>Bodies · Caleb</p>
<h3>“I'm a guy that works. I like to work.”</h3>
<p>“He's always been my accident-prone kid,” Caleb's mother told me as we ate Wendy's burgers in the Turners' living room. When he was suspended from school, his father told him, “If you don't want to be in school, learn with this shovel.” Clearing rocks from the family's blasted foundation, he learned from necessity how to make a pulley.</p>
<p>After the Marines, Caleb became a plumber because a master plumber saw that he was a “natural” with a mechanic's feel. Three years into the apprenticeship, a set of toenailed stairs collapsed beneath him and his 350-pound boss landed on top of him. The nerve pain never left. “I'd get down to my truck, and I'd start it up to leave, shut it off, and go back to the house. Day's not even started, and I'm already hurting.” The work his body made possible became the work his body could no longer endure.</p>
</article>
<article class='story-method-card'>
<p class='story-method-card__type'>Pedagogy · Maya</p>
<h3>“Think with my hands”</h3>
<p>At school, the eleventh-grade building-trades students had been grounded to desk work. Two teachers and I struggled with the bickering, thrown pencils, and calls for help as they wrote CVs. At the field site, the same students dispersed through the unfinished home with a seriousness and organization that shocked me. They told me, “I get bored in everything else,” “I do martial arts and it's the one that would let me work with my body,” and, pointing at a friend, “he's terrible sitting down.”</p>
<p>Maya's mother, after a life in “poverty,” wanted all three of her children to go to college. Maya knew she struggled in “normal school.” She chose building trades because, as she put it, “I figured this is how I could, you know, think with my hands.” Driving back to the College, I regretted not telling her that this was not a lesser thinking.</p>
</article>
<article class='story-method-card'>
<p class='story-method-card__type'>Housing and wages · Tom</p>
<h3>“We don't even have to say what has to get done”</h3>
<p>At 67, Tom told me he regretted the years he had wasted behind a desk. He had moved into the higher-paying managerial side of building, but it left him “frustrated at the boredom, frustrated at life.” After firing all ten of his employees, he returned to the field with his best friend. The two sixty-year-olds “talk without having to talk”: “We show up at the site, and we don't even have to say what has to get done that day. We just do it.”</p>
<p>They had just renovated an antique barn into high-end housing. “Ain't it beautiful?” Tom asked as he moved through the photographs. Next, he hoped to build smaller homes for working families, earn enough to support his daughter, and eventually repair his own cabins for fun. Better pay pulled him toward management; his idea of a good life pulled him back to the field.</p>
</article>
</div>
<div class='story-chapter__heading story-chapter__heading--after-vignettes'>
<p>Throughout the fieldwork I saw constraints—the main ones named here around wages and housing, pedagogy, and workers' bodies—in combat with a shared aspiration which, following Harper, I call a working-knowledge ideal of the good. Working knowledge joins hand and mind in solving practical problems; it is not simply a technique but part of the life people hope work will make possible.</p>
</div>

<p class='story-kicker'>A longer history</p>
<h2>The work ethic has deep and troubled roots</h2>
<p>The region's work ethic is tied to kinship, to stony land, and to the history of settlement on Abenaki land. Local accounts praise hard work, but that history cannot be separated from the colonial violence that made settlement possible. The paper keeps both in view rather than treating work ethic as an innocent regional trait.</p>

      <div class='story-copy'>
        <p class='story-kicker'>4. Towards better structures</p>
        <p>The estate of housing production and regulation is not building enough. A trade labor shortage in its builders' quarters is one reason why. Housing and wages, pedagogy, and workers' bodies compete with the working-knowledge ideal that pulls people into the trades.</p>
        <p>It is not obvious that aspirations towards the good are always against constraints. In zoning meetings I frequently saw them dancing cooperatively together. Homeowners' ideals of a quiet neighborhood, town character, or a white picket fence aligned with a voting logic that restricted supply and protected home values. They could vote, comment in public, and participate in making the ordinances and bylaws they later inhabited. Their ideals and constraints were in sync partly because they had helped create the structure.</p>
        <p>Some constraints are more amenable to change than others, and education looks to be the most amenable. The trade-school program was oversubscribed even after losing much of its usable space, while funding remained poorly matched to the higher costs of teaching building trades. Those are policy decisions. Capacity can be restored, programs can be funded according to what they actually cost, and more students who want to think with their hands can be given room to do so.</p>
        <p>What would it look like for the other relationships to change as well—for builders to participate in their work as zoners do on planning boards, for wages sufficient to live in the region not to pull them away from the field, and for workers' bodies not to be so routinely spent? What could a structure look like in which aspirations towards the good and the constraints of building enough homes were brought into greater harmony?</p>
